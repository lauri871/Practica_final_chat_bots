227GG5W) CARTHAGE. Circa 300-264 BC. AE Shekel(?) (21 mm, 7.67 g). Carthage or Sardinia mint. Wreathed head of Tanit left / Head of horse right; uncertain control mark or letter to right. CNP 252t (Sardinia); MAA 57f (Carthage); SNG Copenhagen 154–5 (Sardinia). Brown-green patina. Some pitting; VF.
 
 
Historical Notes:
This bronze coin was minted during the final decades of Carthaginian dominance in the western Mediterranean, just before the outbreak of the First Punic War (264–241 BC) against Rome. The attribution to either Carthage or Sardinia reflects the broad reach of Carthaginian influence, which extended across North Africa, Sicily, and parts of southern Europe.
• The obverse features Tanit, the principal goddess of Carthage, associated with fertility, protection, and the moon. Her wreathed portrait reflects Hellenistic artistic influence and underscores her central role in Punic religion and civic identity.
• The reverse horse head is one of the most iconic symbols of Carthaginian coinage. It likely represents the famed Numidian cavalry or the sacred status of horses in Punic culture. Horses were vital to Carthage’s military success and trade networks.
• The uncertain control mark beside the horse may indicate a specific minting authority or workshop, though its exact meaning remains debated among scholars.
• If minted in Sardinia, this coin would have served both as currency and a political statement, asserting Carthaginian control over the island’s strategic ports and resources. Sardinia was a contested territory, and coinage helped reinforce Carthage’s presence.
• The dating places this issue in the pre-Hannibalic era, when Carthage was a powerful maritime empire rivaling Rome. These coins circulated widely and were used to pay mercenaries, fund trade, and support colonial administration.
This AE Shekel stands as a tangible link to Carthage’s imperial ambitions and its rich blend of Punic and Hellenistic traditions—an artifact from the brink of one of antiquity’s greatest conflicts.
